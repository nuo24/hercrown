### John 3:16: Explain easier

**Simple explanation.** Jesus has just spoken to Nicodemus about the Son of Man being lifted up, so that everyone who believes may have eternal life. This verse tells why: God loved the world, which was in a ruined, condemned state and unable to rescue itself, and gave His only Son [Clarke]. The gift shows a love that can only be measured by what it gave [JFB]. The result for the one who believes is rescue from perishing and the gift of everlasting life [Clarke]. John goes on to say the Son was sent to save the world, not to condemn it [JFB].

**Key takeaway.** God's love gave His Son so that everyone who believes is saved from perishing and given eternal life.


### John 3:16: Deeper study

**Historical context.** Jesus is speaking to Nicodemus, a Pharisee and member of Israel's ruling council, who came to Him by night. Their conversation moves from the new birth to God's love for the world. Jesus has just compared His coming lifting up to Moses lifting up the snake in the wilderness, so that everyone who believes may have eternal life. Verse 16 explains the love behind that gift, and the verses that follow say God sent the Son to save the world, not to condemn it [JFB] [Clarke].

**Original language.** The adverb *houtōs* ("thus," "so") carries the weight of the sentence; Clarke says Jesus put "an eternity of meaning" in this little word and left it for endless wonder [Clarke]. The verb *ēgapēsen* ("loved," aorist) points to a completed act of love, and *monogenē* ("only begotten," "unique") describes the Son as God's one and only [Lexicon]. The participle *pisteuōn* ("is believing," present) shows faith as ongoing trust in Him [Lexicon].

**Life in biblical times.** The sources don't describe the customs behind this verse.

**Related passages.** Romans 5:8 and 1 John 4:9–10 both trace God's love to the gift of His Son, echoing the same movement from love to giving [Cross-ref]. Romans 8:32 uses the language of not sparing His own Son, which JFB connects directly to this verse [JFB] [Cross-ref]. John 3:15 states the same promise of eternal life for everyone who believes, just before verse 16 repeats it [Cross-ref].

### Psalm 23:1: Explain easier

**Simple explanation.** David takes a picture his readers knew well: a shepherd's care for his flock. Matthew Henry notes that David had himself been a shepherd, so he understood what sheep need and what a faithful shepherd gives. From God being his shepherd, David draws the conclusion that he will lack nothing truly good for him. Henry says more is implied than stated: not only "I shall not want," but "I shall be supplied with whatever I need," and if something desired is withheld, it is either not fit or not good, or will come in due time [Henry].

**Key takeaway.** Because the Lord is our shepherd, we can trust that we will be supplied with whatever we truly need [Henry].


### Psalm 23:1: Deeper study

**Historical context.** David is the speaker in this psalm, and he draws on his own experience as a shepherd to describe God's care for him. Matthew Henry notes that David was taken from following the ewes great with young, so he knew firsthand the cares and tender affections of a good shepherd toward his flock, and how much the sheep needed a skilful and faithful one [Henry]. From the premise that the Lord is his shepherd, David draws the conclusion that he will not lack anything good for him [Henry].

**Original language.** The verse uses יְהוָ֥ה (Yah.weh), the proper name rendered "LORD" [Lexicon]. The word רֹ֝עִ֗י (ro.'I) is a qal participle meaning "shepherd" or "to pasture," so God is described as actively tending His people [Lexicon]. The phrase לֹא אֶחְסָֽר (lo' 'ech.Sar) uses a qal imperfect verb meaning "to lack," giving the sense "I shall not lack" [Lexicon].

**Life in biblical times.** The sources don't describe the customs behind this verse.

**Related passages.** In John 10:11, Jesus calls Himself the good shepherd who lays down His life for the sheep, echoing the shepherd image here [Cross-ref]. ### Hebrews 11:1: Explain easier

**Simple explanation.** Hebrews 11:1 describes faith in two ways. Faith is "the substance of things hoped for" — the same things we hope for are what we believe, and that persuasion gives the soul a kind of present possession and foretaste of them [Henry]. Clarke says the word behind "substance" means a subsistence, a foundation for something else to stand on [Clarke]. Faith is also "the evidence of things not seen": it brings the reality of invisible things before the mind, serving the believer instead of sight [Henry]. The next verses show this faith is old, commended in the ancients, and holds that the universe was formed at God's command [Henry].

**Key takeaway.** Faith gives present substance to what we hope for and makes real to the soul what the eyes cannot see.


### Hebrews 11:1: Deeper study

**Historical context.** The letter to the Hebrews is written to believers who came to faith from a Jewish background, and it urges them to hold fast when following Christ costs them something. Here the writer turns from warning to encouragement, opening a long section on faith by describing what faith itself is. He points back to "the ancients" (Hebrews 11:2) and forward to creation (Hebrews 11:3), treating faith as the thread running through every generation of God's people [Henry].

**Original language.** Two nouns carry the weight. *Hupostasis* [Lexicon], translated "assurance" or "substance," means a foundation or subsistence — that which stands under a thing and holds it up [Clarke]. *Elegchos* [Lexicon], "conviction" or "evidence," is a demonstration so convincing that no doubt remains [Clarke]. Both describe faith as giving the soul a present, solid hold on what is not yet seen [Henry].

**Life in biblical times.** The sources don't describe the customs behind this verse.

When the people cried out, God raised up Deborah. She was a prophetess, the wife of Lappidoth, and she judged Israel at that time [JFB]. She held the people's respect and carried out the duties of a judge except military leadership [JFB]. The northern tribes, nearest the oppressor, especially cried to God [Henry].

**Original language.** נְבִיאָה (ne.vi.'Ah) means "prophetess," a woman instructed in divine knowledge by the Spirit [Lexicon][JFB]. שֹׁפְטָה (sho.fe.Tah) is an active participle, "was judging," describing her ongoing work of judgment [Lexicon]. אֵשֶׁת (E.shet) means "wife of," linking her here to Lappidoth [Lexicon].

**Life in biblical times.** The sources don't describe the customs behind this verse.

**Related passages.** Exodus 15:20 names Miriam a prophetess, showing Deborah was not the only woman so called [Cross-ref]. 2 Kings 22:14 describes Huldah as a prophetess and a wife, much like Deborah [Cross-ref]. Luke 2:36 mentions Anna, a prophetess, continuing this pattern [Cross-ref].

### Galatians 3:28: Explain easier

**Simple explanation.** Paul has just said that believers are God's sons through faith and have clothed themselves with Christ (verses 26–27). Now he spells out what that means: the old rankings no longer decide who is welcome. Being Jewish or Gentile, enslaved or free, male or female, makes no difference to what Christ gives. [JFB] says no class is privileged above another as the Jews once were under the law, and that difference of sex makes no difference in Christian privileges. [Clarke] puts it plainly: all are equally welcome to Christ, all equally need him, and all who believe become one family through him.

**Key takeaway.** In Christ, every old division loses its power to rank us; all who believe belong equally and are made one.


### Galatians 3:28: Deeper study

**Historical context.** Paul is writing to the churches in Galatia, addressing believers who had come to faith in Christ. In the surrounding verses he explains that faith has come and that those who believe are sons of God and have clothed themselves with Christ through baptism [JFB]. The verse sits within his argument that, in this sonship by faith, no class is privileged above another as the Jews had been above the Gentiles under the law [JFB]. He is describing the new standing believers share in Christ.

**Original language.** The word *heis* (one) is an adjective, nominative singular masculine, meaning "one" [Lexicon]. Jamieson, Fausset & Brown note it is masculine rather than neuter, namely "one new man" in Christ [JFB]. The verse repeats *ouk eni* ("there is not") three times, and *doulos* (slave) and *eleutheros* (free) name the social pair set aside [Lexicon].

**Life in biblical times.** Under the law the male sex held great privileges: males alone bore circumcision, the sign of the covenant, and alone could be kings and priests, with prior right to inheritances [JFB]. Clarke lists further disparities—a man could shave his head and rend his clothes in mourning, impose vows, betroth or sell a daughter, while a woman could not, and women were treated in many cases more like children than adults [Clarke].

**Related passages.** Colossians 3:11 echoes this verse, naming Greek and Jew, slave and free, with Christ as all and in all [Cross-ref]. First Corinthians 12:12–13 describes believers baptized into one body, whether Jews or Greeks, slave or free [Cross-ref]. Romans 10:12–15 declares there is no difference between Jew and Greek, since the same Lord is Lord of all [Cross-ref].

**Reflect.** Where do you find it hardest to believe that another believer stands on exactly the same footing before Christ as you do?
