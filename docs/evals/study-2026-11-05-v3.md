### Psalm 23:1: Deeper study

**Biblical meaning.** Psalm 23 opens with a confession of trust: "The LORD is my shepherd; I shall not want." The psalm then unfolds what that shepherding looks like — rest in green pastures, guidance beside quiet waters, restoration of the soul, and paths of righteousness for God's name's sake (Psalm 23:2–3). Matthew Henry reads the psalm as drawing "three very comfortable conclusions" from "three very comfortable premises," and the first conclusion is that because God is the speaker's shepherd, he will lack nothing that is good for him [Henry].

**Historical context.** The psalm is attributed to David, who had himself been a shepherd before becoming king. Henry notes that David was "taken from following the ewes great with young" (Psalm 78:70–71), so he knew from experience "the cares and tender affections of a good shepherd towards his flock," including the risk of his life to rescue a lamb. Henry also observes that David wrote from a place of real need — once sending men to ask for help from Nabal, and once going himself to Ahimelech — yet could still say he would not want [Henry].

**Original language.** The verse uses יְהוָה (Yah.weh), the proper name rendered "LORD," and רֹעִי (ro.'i), a qal participle meaning "shepherd" or "one who pastures" — "my shepherd." The verb אֶחְסָר ('ech.Sar) is a first-person imperfect from a root meaning "to lack," so "I shall not want" carries the sense "I will not lack" [Lexicon].

**Theological significance.** Henry grounds the psalm's confidence in the relationship itself: God is the shepherd, and the believer may call him so. From that relation David concludes he "shall not want any thing that is really necessary and good for me." Henry adds that more is implied than expressed — not only "I shall not want," but "I shall be supplied with whatever I need," and if something desired is withheld, it is either not fit, not good, or will come in due time. The text states the confidence; Henry's conclusion about withheld desires is his interpretation [Henry].

**Related passages.** John 10:11 records Jesus saying, "I am the good shepherd," which Henry connects to this psalm's shepherd imagery [Henry][Cross-ref]. Isaiah 40:11 pictures God tending His flock, gathering lambs in His arms and gently leading the nursing ewes [Cross-ref]. Philippians 4:19 promises that God will supply all your needs according to His riches in Christ Jesus [Cross-ref].

**Reflect.** Where in your life right now do you most need to hear "I shall not want" — and what makes it hard to believe?

**Key takeaway.** Faith gives what we hope for a present footing in the soul and makes unseen realities certain to the mind.


### Hebrews 11:1: Deeper study

**Biblical meaning.** Hebrews 11 opens by describing faith itself before naming anyone who lived by it. Faith is tied to hope: the same things we hope for are what we believe, and this persuasion gives the soul a present taste of what is promised [Henry]. Faith also makes real to the mind what the eye cannot see, serving the believer in place of sight [Henry]. Verse 2 then says the ancients were commended for this, and verse 3 names creation by God's command as a first act of faith [Henry].

**Historical context.** The letter is written to believers who are being urged to persevere, and chapter 11 gathers examples of faith from the past. The writer speaks of "the ancients" and "the elders" — believers who lived in the first ages of the world — and says their faith was their honor, reflecting credit on them and receiving a good report [Henry]. The creation of the worlds by God's word is presented as an article of faith common to believers in every age and place [Henry].

**Original language.** The word ὑπόστασις (hupostasis) is translated "substance" or "assurance"; it means subsistence, that which becomes a foundation for another thing to stand on [Clarke]. The word ἔλεγχος (elegchos) is rendered "evidence" or "conviction"; it means such a conviction as is produced in the mind by demonstration, after which no doubt remains [Clarke]. Both are nouns describing what faith itself is [Lexicon].

**Theological significance.** Faith and hope belong together: faith is a firm persuasion and expectation that God will perform what he has promised in Christ, strong enough to give the soul a kind of possession and foretaste of those things [Henry]. Faith also assents to God's revelation and sets its seal that God is true, making application of what God has revealed to the soul with suitable affections and endeavors [Henry]. Clarke describes justifying faith as producing peace with God, the love of God shed abroad in the heart, and the Spirit's witness that sins are blotted out [Clarke]. These are the commentators' explanations of the text's claims, not the text's own wording.

**Related passages.** 2 Corinthians 5:7 states that we walk by faith, not by sight, matching the verse's contrast between faith and visible things [Cross-ref]. Romans 8:24–25 notes that hope that is seen is no hope at all, and that we wait patiently for what we do not yet see [Cross-ref]. Hebrews 11:7 gives Noah as an example of faith responding to things not yet seen [Cross-ref].

**Reflect.** Where in your life right now are you being asked to treat what God has promised as more solid than what you can see?

### Jeremiah 29:11: Deeper study

**Biblical meaning.** The verse sits inside a letter Jeremiah sends to the exiles, and it answers the false hope around it. Verses 8 and 9 warn against prophets and diviners promising a quick return; verse 10 sets the real timeline at seventy years. Only then does God say He knows His plans for them. The contrast is deliberate: the false prophets know nothing of God's purposes, while He alone knows them [JFB]. The plans are for well-being, not harm, and they end in a future and a hope [JFB].

**Historical context.** God is speaking through Jeremiah to the Jews already carried away to Babylon. The people had been told by self-appointed prophets that the captivity would be short, so they should stay ready to leave rather than settle down [Henry]. God corrects that expectation and tells them the return will come only after the seventy years are complete [Henry]. The promise is that He will visit them in mercy, end the captivity, and gather them back to their own land [Henry].

**Original language.** *ki* ("for") opens the sentence as a grounding particle [Lexicon]. *ya.Da'.ti*, "I know," is a first-person verb meaning to know [Lexicon]. *mach.she.Vot*, "plans," is a feminine plural noun glossed "plot," and *sha.lOm* is a masculine noun meaning "peace: well-being" [Lexicon]. The verse ends with *'a.cha.Rit*, "a future" (also "end"), and *ve.tik.Vah*, "and a hope" [Lexicon].

**Theological significance.** The text presents God as the one whose purposes stand, in contrast to the prophets who claimed to speak for Him but did not [JFB]. The moral problem the commentary names is twofold: first a vain confidence that everything will turn out well, then, when that fails, a despair that cannot imagine deliverance at all [JFB]. The promise of an "expected end" is literally an end and an expectation—an outcome such as the people wished for [JFB]. What the verse states is God's intention toward the exiles; the commentary draws out that His timing, not theirs, is the reliable one [Henry].

**Related passages.** Isaiah 55:8–12 is cited by the commentary alongside this verse, contrasting God's thoughts with human thoughts [JFB]. Psalm 33:11 and Job 23:13 both stress that God's counsel and purposes stand and that He does what He desires [Cross-ref]. Lamentations 3:26 adds the posture of waiting quietly for the LORD's salvation [Cross-ref].

**Reflect.** Where in your own waiting are you tempted to trade patient trust for either a rushed hope or a settled despair?

### Matthew 7:1: Explain easier

### Matthew 7:1: Deeper study

**Biblical meaning.** Jesus is teaching His disciples how to treat the faults of others. The command "Do not judge" is paired with a warning: the same judgment we pronounce will be measured back to us [Henry]. The next verses show the picture — noticing a speck in a brother's eye while missing the beam in your own [Cross-ref]. The point is not that all evaluation is wrong, but that harsh, self-exalting condemnation of others invites the same measure in return [Henry].

**Historical context.** Jesus is speaking to His disciples in the Sermon on the Mount. Henry reads these words as a reproof to the scribes and Pharisees, who were severe and supercilious in condemning everyone around them while justifying themselves [Henry]. The caution is directed to private persons, not to magistrates or ministers whose office includes judging; Christ did not unmake those roles [Henry]. It addresses how disciples conduct themselves toward others' faults [Henry].

**Original language.** The verb κρίνετε (krinete) means "to judge," a present active imperative, second person plural — a ongoing command to "you all" [Lexicon]. It is paired with κριθῆτε (krithēte), "you may be judged," an aorist passive subjunctive [Lexicon]. The passive form matters: the judging we do returns upon us. Both are framed by μή (mē), "not," marking the prohibition [Lexicon].

**Theological significance.** Henry explains the reason for the prohibition: if we presume to judge others, we may expect to be judged ourselves — by people, and ultimately by God, before whom both parties must appear [Henry]. He also notes the reverse: if we are modest and charitable, declining to judge others and judging ourselves instead, we will not be judged by the Lord [Henry]. The text states a measure-for-measure principle; the commentary draws out both the human and divine dimensions of that warning [Henry].

**Related passages.** Luke 6:37 repeats the command and links it with forgiving [Cross-ref]. Romans 2:1–2 warns that judging another on grounds we ourselves share condemns us [Cross-ref]. James 4:11–12 explains that speaking against a brother puts us in the seat of the one Lawgiver and Judge [Cross-ref]. Each passage reinforces the same caution against taking a role that belongs to God.

**Reflect.** Where in your life are you measuring others by a standard you would not want measured back to you?


### Isaiah 40:31: Explain easier

### Isaiah 40:31: Deeper study

**Biblical meaning.** Isaiah 40:31 closes a series of contrasts. Verses 28–30 declare that the LORD, the everlasting Creator, does not grow tired, while even youths grow weary and stumble. Verse 31 turns that around: those who wait on the LORD will renew their strength. The verse then moves through a descending climax—mount up, run, walk—so that in every posture the waiting person is sustained [JFB]. The imagery of eagles and renewed strength carries the thought of strength restored rather than merely summoned [JFB].

**Historical context.** The available sources do not give enough detail for this section.

**Original language.** The participle וְקוֹיֵ (ve.ko.Ye) means "and those who wait for," from a verb meaning to await—an active, ongoing posture rather than a single act [Lexicon]. יַחֲלִיפוּ (ya.cha.Li.fu), "they will renew," is a hiphil imperfect, expressing caused or exchanged strength [Lexicon]. יַעֲלוּ (ya.'a.Lu) means "they will go up," paired with אֵבֶר (E.ver), "wing," and כַּנְּשָׁרִים (ka.ne.sha.Rim), "like the eagles" [Lexicon].

**Theological significance.** The passage grounds human renewal in God's own unwearying nature: He gives power to the faint and increases the strength of the weak [Cross-ref, Isaiah 40:29]. ### John 11:35: Deeper study

**Biblical meaning.** Jesus arrives to find Mary and the Jews with her weeping over Lazarus's death. Mary falls at His feet, and Jesus is deeply moved in spirit and troubled [JFB]. In this moment of shared grief, He weeps. The verse's brevity is striking: two short words convey silent tears rather than the loud wailing of the mourners around Him [JFB]. Immediately after, onlookers say, "See how He loved him!" while others wonder why He could not have prevented the death [Cross-ref]. The verse sits at the center of the account, showing His compassion before He raises Lazarus.

**Historical context.** Jesus is speaking with Mary, sister of Lazarus, outside Bethany after Lazarus has died. Mary and the Jews who came with her are weeping for the loss of a brother and friend [Henry]. Jesus sees their grief and asks where Lazarus has been laid [Cross-ref]. The Evangelist John records this scene some sixty years after it occurred, holding up the spectacle of the Son of God in tears [JFB]. The onlookers interpret His weeping as a sign of love for Lazarus [Cross-ref].

**Original language.** The verb is ἐδάκρυσεν (edakrusen), meaning "wept" — to weep, in the aorist active indicative, third person singular [Lexicon]. The subject is Ἰησοῦς (Iēsous), "Jesus," a nominative singular noun [Lexicon]. The word here denotes silent tears, distinct from the loud wailing for the dead used twice in John 11:33 [JFB].

**Life in biblical times.** The sources describe weeping for the dead as a customary response to loss. Mary and the Jews wept for a loving brother and a good neighbor and friend [Henry]. Nature itself teaches people to weep over dear relations removed by death, and Providence calls to weeping and mourning [Henry]. Those who love their friends share in their joys and griefs [Henry].

**Theological significance.** The verse shows Christ's tender sympathy with His afflicted friends and the share He took in their sorrows [Henry]. His tears seal His perfect oneness with us in our stricken humanity [JFB]. Some interpreters ask whether His tears went beyond sorrow for human suffering and death, suggesting that in His ear the scene proclaimed the law that "the wages of sin is death" (Rom 6:23) [JFB]. Others emphasize His genuine, inward affection — not counterfeited sympathy but a deep and hearty sigh [Henry]. The text itself simply says He wept; the interpretations differ on what lay beneath those tears.

Hebrews 4:15 speaks of a high priest who sympathizes with our weaknesses and was tempted in every way yet without sin [Cross-ref]. John 11:33 describes Jesus being deeply moved in spirit and troubled just before this verse [Cross-ref].

### 1 Peter 5:7: Explain easier

The casting is decisive and done once for all, as the Greek tense shows [JFB]. The burden of care is lifted off the person and placed on God [JFB]. God concerns Himself with whatever touches His people, spiritual or temporal, great or small; what affects them affects Him [Clarke]. Peter echoes Psalm 55:22, where the burden is cast on the Lord who sustains [Clarke].

**Key takeaway.** Because God genuinely concerns Himself with everything that touches you, you can hand Him your anxiety and stop carrying it alone [Clarke].


### 1 Peter 5:7: Deeper study

**Biblical meaning.** This verse sits between two calls to humility. Peter has just urged his readers to humble themselves under God's mighty hand, promising that in due time He will exalt them [Henry]. The verse then names the practical result of that humility: casting anxiety on God in confident reliance on His goodness [JFB]. The Greek verb is a single, decisive act, "once for all" [JFB]. Peter pictures care as a burden that faith lifts off the person and places on God [JFB]. The word for God's care is a milder, less intense Greek term than the word for our anxiety [JFB]. The next verses turn to watchfulness against the devil [Henry].

**Historical context.** The apostle Peter writes to Christians who are "already under very hard circumstances" [Henry]. Earlier in the letter he has addressed the pastors or spiritual guides of the church, and now he turns to instruct the flock, including the younger among them [Henry]. The passage speaks to people facing suffering, since the following verses mention brothers throughout the world undergoing the same kinds of suffering [Henry]. Into that setting Peter speaks about anxiety and humility, not as abstract ideas but as duties for a pressured community [Henry].

**Original language.** The noun μέριμναν (merimnan) means "anxiety" or "concern," and it is the burden Peter tells his readers to cast [Lexicon]. The verb ἐπιρίψαντες (epiripsantes), "having cast," means "to throw on," and it is an aorist participle, matching the "once for all" force noted in the commentary [Lexicon][JFB]. The verb μέλει (melei), "there is care," means "to concern," and it is present tense, describing God's ongoing concern for His people [Lexicon].

**Theological significance.** The text ties humility to freedom from anxiety: exemption from care goes along with humble submission to God [JFB]. Peter alludes to Psalm 55:22, where the burden is cast on the Lord who sustains the one who casts it [Clarke]. Clarke draws out that whatever concerns a follower of God, spiritual or temporal, great or small, God concerns Himself with it, and what affects them affects Him [Clarke]. The verse states God's care as its reason; the commentaries press further, concluding that the person who knows God cares need not be anxious about themselves [Clarke]. That is an inference drawn from the text rather than the text's own words.

**Related passages.** Psalm 55:22 supplies the image Peter echoes, casting a burden on the Lord who sustains [Cross-ref][Clarke]. Philippians 4:6 turns the same release into practice, replacing anxiety with prayer and petition offered with thanksgiving [Cross-ref]. Matthew 6:25–26 grounds the command not to worry in the Father's care for birds and His greater value for people [Cross-ref].

**Simple explanation.** Psalm 46 pictures a world of upheaval, yet the people of God are called to stop striving and to know that He is God. ### Psalm 46:10: Deeper study

**Biblical meaning.** Psalm 46 moves from God as refuge in trouble to a call to look at what He does among the nations. Verses 8–9 invite the reader to see the LORD bringing devastation and ending wars, breaking bow, spear and shields. Verse 10 follows that display of power: the command to be still and know that He is God, and the promise that He will be exalted among the nations and over the earth. Verse 11 repeats the refrain that the LORD of Hosts is with us and the God of Jacob is our fortress [JFB].

**Historical context.** The sources do not name the human author, the occasion, or the original audience of Psalm 46. What the nearby verses show is a song of trust spoken to a community facing threat: the nations rage and wars exist, yet the LORD of Hosts is present with His people and is their fortress [JFB]. The command in verse 10 is set within that situation of conflict, addressed to those who oppose God and vex His people [JFB].

**Original language.** הַרְפּוּ (har.Pu) is a plural imperative meaning "be quiet," from a root meaning to slacken [Lexicon]. וּדְעוּ (U.de.'u) is a plural imperative, "and know," from the verb to know [Lexicon]. Together they are commands given to a group, not a private suggestion. אָרוּם ('a.Rum), "I will be exalted," is first person singular, qal imperfect, and appears twice, once with בַּגּוֹיִם, "among the nations," and once with בָּאָרֶץ, "on the earth" [Lexicon].

So the stillness called for is not merely calm; it is the ceasing of opposition to God. The verse also states God's own resolve: He will be exalted among the nations and over the earth [Lexicon]. The nearby verses ground this in His action of ending wars and breaking weapons [JFB]. The text presents God's exaltation as something He Himself will accomplish, not something His people must achieve for Him.

**Related passages.** Isaiah 2:17 says the pride of man will be brought low and the LORD alone will be exalted in that day, matching the theme of God's exaltation [Cross-ref]. Habakkuk 2:20 calls all the earth to be silent before the LORD, echoing the command to be still [Cross-ref]. Ezekiel 38:23 says God will reveal Himself in the sight of many nations, and then they will know that He is the LORD, connecting to "know that I am God" [Cross-ref].

### Micah 6:8: Explain easier

**Simple explanation.** Micah 6:8 answers the questions of verses 6–7, where Israel asks what grand offering could make peace with God. The prophet says God has already shown them what is good, so the question need not be asked as if they had never heard [JFB]. What God requires is not endless sacrifice but justice, mercy, and humble walking with him [JFB]. Henry notes that convicted people ask what they must do to please God, and Clarke reads the three duties as giving God, neighbour, and self their due, loving kindness, and humbling ourselves before God [Henry][Clarke].

**Key takeaway.** God has already shown what he asks: do justly, love mercy, and walk humbly with him.


### Micah 6:8: Deeper study

**Biblical meaning.** Micah 6:8 answers the questions of verses 6–7, where someone asks what would be enough to come before God—burnt offerings, year-old calves, thousands of rams, rivers of oil, even a firstborn child [Henry]. The answer turns from sacrifice to what God has already made known: justice, mercy, and humble walking with God [JFB]. The prophet presents these moral duties as the ends that the positive ordinances of sacrifice were only meant to serve [JFB]. The verse is the verdict on Israel's inquiry, not a new demand but a reminder of what was shown long ago [JFB].

**Historical context.** The passage is a controversy between God and Israel, and Israel has been convicted of injustice and ingratitude toward God [Henry]. The people, made sensible of the justice of God's case against them, ask the prophet—the Lord's messenger who convinced them—what they might do to be reconciled to God [Henry]. Each person speaks for himself: "What shall I do?" rather than asking what someone else should do [Henry]. They ask what they may bring, since they must not appear before the Lord empty [Henry].

**Original language.** *chesed* (חֶסֶד) is rendered "covenant loyalty" and "kindness"; here it is the object of "to love," so the verse calls for a love of kindness or mercy [Lexicon]. *mishpat* (מִשְׁפָּט) means "justice," the object of "to do" [Lexicon]. The verb behind "to walk humbly," *hatzne'a* (הַצְנֵעַ), is glossed "be humble" or "be modest," joined to "to walk" with God [Lexicon].

**Theological significance.** The commentators read the three duties as moral obligations of lasting weight, preferred by God to sacrifice, which was a means designed with those duties in view [JFB]. Justice is strict equity; mercy is a kindly abatement of what we might justly demand and a hearty desire to do good to others; walking humbly with God is passive and active obedience toward Him [JFB]. Clarke likewise ties justice to giving God, neighbor, and self their due, and mercy to kindness and charity beyond what justice requires [Clarke]. Interpreters differ: JFB reads the "good" as revealed in connection with the good things to come under Messiah, while Clarke stresses that these duties do not merit salvation and that humility means submitting to be saved by God's free mercy [JFB][Clarke].

**Related passages.** Deuteronomy 10:12–13 asks what the Lord requires—fearing Him, walking in His ways, loving Him, serving Him, and keeping His commandments—close in shape to Micah's answer [Cross-ref]. Hosea 6:6 and 1 Samuel 15:22 both set mercy and obedience above sacrifice, matching the contrast Micah draws [Cross-ref]. Proverbs 21:3 says doing righteousness and justice is more desirable to the Lord than sacrifice [Cross-ref].

### 1 Timothy 2:12: Deeper study

**Biblical meaning.** In this section of his letter, Paul addresses how women should conduct themselves in the gathered church. He has just asked that they dress modestly and learn quietly [Henry]. ### Galatians 3:28: Explain easier

**Simple explanation.** Paul has just said that all who believe are sons of God, clothed with Christ in baptism. Now he says the old divisions no longer count. Jamieson, Fausset and Brown read this as saying that in this sonship by faith no class is privileged above another, the way Jews once stood above Gentiles [JFB]. Clarke says all distinctions are done away as either helping or hindering; all are equally welcome to Christ and equally need him, and believers of every background and sex become one family through him [Clarke]. The word "one" is masculine in Greek, "one man," pointing to one new man in Christ [JFB].

**Key takeaway.** In Christ, differences of background, status and sex give no one an advantage and exclude no one from equal standing.


### Galatians 3:28: Deeper study

**Biblical meaning.** The verse stands at the close of Paul's argument about faith and sonship. Just before it, he says that faith has come and believers are no longer under a guardian, that they are all sons of God through faith, and that those baptized into Christ have clothed themselves with Christ [JFB]. Into that flow of thought he places this statement: the old rankings no longer define who belongs. The commentaries read it as a declaration that no class is privileged above another, as the Jews once were above the Gentiles under the law [JFB]. The point is unity in Christ, not the erasure of who people are.

**Historical context.** Paul writes to the churches of Galatia, addressing a dispute over whether Gentile believers must take up the law and its markers to belong. The surrounding verses speak of a guardian, of sonship through faith, and of baptism as clothing oneself with Christ [JFB]. The verse names three divisions familiar in that world: Jew and Greek, slave and free, male and female. The commentaries note that under the law the Jews held a privileged standing above the Gentiles, and that males held privileges women did not [JFB][Clarke].

**Original language.** The word εἷς (heis, "one") is masculine, not neuter — the commentaries take it as "one new man" in Christ [JFB][Lexicon]. The phrase rendered "neither male nor female" is literally "there is not male and female," using ἄρσεν (arsen) and θῆλυ (thēlu) [Lexicon]. The verb ἔνι (eni, "there is") repeats with each pair, and ὑμεῖς (humeis, "you yourselves") with ἐστε (este, "are") grounds the unity in the readers [Lexicon].

**Life in biblical times.** The commentaries describe disparities in Jewish practice: a man could shave his head and tear his clothes in mourning, impose a nazirite vow on his son, be shorn for his father's vow, betroth his daughter, or sell his daughter; a woman could do none of these [Clarke]. Women were not permitted to assemble with the men in the synagogues but were placed in galleries [Clarke]. Males alone bore circumcision, the sign of the covenant, and alone could be kings and priests [JFB].

**Theological significance.** The verse ties belonging entirely to faith in Christ rather than to birth, status, or sex. The commentaries say that in this sonship by faith no class is privileged above another, and that Christ alike belongs to slave and free by faith [JFB]. Baptism, unlike circumcision, was applied to male and female alike, and all of either sex are now "kings and priests unto God" [JFB]. The text states unity in Christ; the commentaries draw out that this makes no difference to Christian privileges, while noting that in the resurrection the relation of the sexes shall cease [JFB].

**Related passages.** 1 Corinthians 12:12–13 speaks of many parts forming one body, baptized into one body whether Jews or Greeks, slave or free [Cross-ref]. Colossians 3:11 lists similar divisions and says Christ is all and is in all [Cross-ref]. Romans 10:12–15 declares no difference between Jew and Greek, since the same Lord gives richly to all who call on Him [Cross-ref].
