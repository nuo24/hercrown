### John 3:16: Explain easier

**Simple explanation.** Jesus has just spoken to Nicodemus about the Son of Man being lifted up, so that everyone who believes may have eternal life [JFB]. John 3:16 explains the source of that salvation: God's love for a world that was in a ruinous, condemned state and unable to rescue itself [Clarke]. That love gave the Son, and the verse names both the rescue and the result—believers are spared from perishing and receive everlasting life [Clarke]. The means by which this takes effect is believing on the Son [JFB].

**Key takeaway.** God's love gave His Son so that everyone who believes is rescued from perishing and given eternal life.


### John 3:16: Deeper study

**Biblical meaning.** Jesus is speaking with Nicodemus, and the verses just before this one set the scene: the Son of Man must be "lifted up," like the snake Moses raised in the wilderness, so that everyone who believes may have eternal life [Cross-ref]. Verse 16 explains the motive behind that lifting up. The picture John paints has distinct parts: a world ready to perish, God's love measured by the gift it produced, the gift itself—His only Son—and the outcome for those who believe: rescue from perishing and the gift of everlasting life [JFB]. The means by which all of this takes effect is believing on the Son [JFB].

**Historical context.** Jesus is speaking to Nicodemus, a Pharisee and member of Israel's ruling council, who came to him at night [JFB]. Nicodemus approached with the narrow outlook of his Jewish training, and Jesus' words about God's love for "the world" would have stretched far beyond what he expected [JFB]. The verse sits within a private conversation, not a public sermon, and it answers Nicodemus' confusion about being born again. Clarke notes that Jesus does not attempt to describe such love—he simply states it and leaves it for endless contemplation [Clarke].

**Original language.** The adverb *houtōs* ("thus," "so") carries the weight here—Clarke says Jesus "put an eternity of meaning" in this small word, leaving a subject for everlasting wonder [Clarke]. The verb *ēgapēsen* ("loved") is aorist, pointing to a definite act of love [Lexicon]. *Monogenē* is rendered "only begotten" or "unique," describing the Son as one of a kind [Lexicon]. The participle *pisteuōn* ("is believing") is present tense, suggesting ongoing trust rather than a single moment [Lexicon].

**Theological significance.** Clarke lays out the state of things plainly: the world was ruined and condemned, unable to rescue itself, and God provided salvation by giving His Son to die for it [Clarke]. He also insists that no one is saved through this sacrifice except the one who believes—who trusts what God has spoken about Christ, His sacrifice, and how it is applied [Clarke]. Those who believe receive a double benefit: exemption from eternal perdition and the gift of eternal glory [Clarke]. JFB frames the same truth as love that can only be measured by the gift it drew forth from God [JFB]. Christians have long read this verse differently on how the scope of "the world" and the means of salvation relate; the text itself states God's love for the world and the condition of believing.

**Related passages.** Romans 5:8 echoes the timing of this love—Christ died for us while we were still sinners [Cross-ref]. 1 John 4:9–10 describes the same sending of the Son and calls it an atoning sacrifice for sins [Cross-ref]. John 3:15, the verse just before, states the same promise in nearly identical terms: everyone who believes may have eternal life [Cross-ref].

**Reflect.** What does it change in your day to know that God's love for the world was already settled before you believed?


### Psalm 23:1: Explain easier

**Simple explanation.** David speaks from experience: he had been a shepherd himself, so he knew the care, skill and faithfulness a good shepherd gives to a flock [Henry]. When he calls the LORD his shepherd, he draws a conclusion from that relationship — that he will not lack anything good for him [Henry]. Henry notes that more is implied than stated: not only "I shall not want," but "I shall be supplied with whatever I need," and if something desired is withheld, it is either not fit or not good, or it will come in due time [Henry].

**Key takeaway.** Because the LORD is our shepherd, we can trust that He will supply what is truly good for us [Henry].


### Psalm 23:1: Deeper study

**Biblical meaning.** Psalm 23 opens with a picture of relationship before it makes any promise. David names the LORD as his shepherd, then draws a conclusion from that name: he will not lack. The verses that follow unfold what this shepherding looks like in practice — rest in green pastures, guidance beside quiet waters, a restored soul, right paths [Henry]. The whole psalm rests on the premise that the shepherd's character settles the sheep's future. Because the LORD stands in this relation to him, David reasons that whatever is truly necessary and good will be supplied, and if something he wants is withheld, it is either not fit for him or will come in due time [Henry].

**Historical context.** David is the speaker, and he writes from his own experience of keeping sheep before he was taken from following the ewes to shepherd Israel [Henry]. He knew firsthand what a flock needed and what it cost a shepherd to provide it, so he reaches for that familiar work to describe God's care for his people [Henry]. Matthew Henry notes that David may have written this psalm before coming to the crown, since he had once sent men to ask Nabal for provisions and had gone himself to Ahimelech for bread — circumstances in which he had real reason to fear want [Henry].

**Original language.** The verse pairs two words that carry the whole weight of the line. *Yah.weh* (יְהוָה) is the divine name, given here as a proper noun, the LORD [Lexicon]. *ro.'I* (רֹעִי) is a qal participle from a verb meaning "to pasture," so it is active and ongoing: "my shepherd," the one continually tending [Lexicon]. Then *'ech.Sar* (אֶחְסָר), a first-person imperfect from "to lack," expresses not a single moment but a continuing reality: I shall not lack [Lexicon].

**Theological significance.** Henry frames the verse as one of three "comfortable premises" from which David draws comfortable conclusions, and he grounds the confidence in God's relation to his people rather than in their circumstances [Henry]. The shepherd imagery is not unique to David; Henry reads it as pointing forward to Christ, who calls himself the good shepherd, and notes that the shepherd of Israel as a whole is also shepherd of each particular believer, so that even the least is not beneath his notice [Henry]. The text itself says the LORD is shepherd and therefore the psalmist will not lack; Henry's conclusion is that this implies not merely the absence of want but the supply of whatever is needed, with unmet desires left to God's judgment about what is fit and timely [Henry].

**Related passages.** John 10:11 records Jesus calling himself the good shepherd who lays down his life for the sheep, the image Henry sees fulfilled here [Henry][Cross-ref]. Isaiah 40:11 shows the same shepherd gathering lambs and carrying them close, gentle with the nursing ewes [Cross-ref]. Philippians 4:19 echoes the "shall not want" conclusion, promising that God supplies needs according to his riches in Christ Jesus [Cross-ref].

**Reflect.** Where in your life are you most tempted to read your circumstances as proof that the shepherd has stopped tending you?


### Hebrews 11:1: Explain easier

**Simple explanation.** Hebrews 11:1 describes faith in two ways. It is the substance of things hoped for, meaning faith and hope share the same object, and faith gives the soul a present grasp of what God has promised [Henry]. It is also the evidence of things not seen, bringing the reality of invisible things before the mind the way the senses serve the body [Henry]. Clarke notes the word behind "substance" means a foundation or subsistence, and the word behind "evidence" means a conviction produced by demonstration, leaving no doubt [Clarke].

**Key takeaway.** Faith gives what we hope for a present footing in the soul and makes unseen realities certain to the mind.


### Hebrews 11:1: Deeper study

**Biblical meaning.** Hebrews 11 opens a long list of faithful people by first describing what faith itself is. The writer calls faith the assurance of what we hope for and the certainty of what we do not see [Henry]. Faith and hope belong together: the same promised things are the object of both, and faith gives them a kind of present substance in the soul [Henry]. Faith also makes real to the mind what the eye cannot see, standing in place of sight for the believer [Henry]. Verse 2 then says the ancients were commended for this, and verse 3 names creation as a first example of faith's reach [Henry].

**Historical context.** The sources don't name the writer or the first readers of Hebrews, and they don't describe the circumstances in which it was written. Henry treats the "ancients" of verse 2 as believers who lived in the first ages of the world, and he calls faith an old grace, practiced from the beginning of revelation rather than a new invention [Henry]. Beyond that, the sources don't describe the setting of the letter.

**Original language.** Two nouns carry the verse. *Hupostasis* is translated "assurance" or "substance"; the lexicon gives its meaning as confidence or essence, and Clarke explains it as a subsistence, that which becomes a foundation for another thing to stand on [Lexicon][Clarke]. *Elegchos* is translated "conviction" or "evidence"; the lexicon gives "rebuke," and Clarke describes it as a conviction produced in the mind by demonstration, after which no doubt can remain [Lexicon][Clarke]. The hoped-for and unseen things are both present passive participles, "things being hoped for" and "things being seen" [Lexicon].

**Theological significance.** Henry and Clarke both stress that faith is not a vague feeling but a firm persuasion. Henry says faith is the soul's firm assent to the divine revelation, setting to its seal that God is true, and that faith which does not make invisible things real to the soul is mere opinion or fancy [Henry]. Clarke describes the conviction faith produces as like a demonstrated proof, and adds that the Spirit witnesses with believers who have this faith that their sins are blotted out [Clarke]. The text itself defines faith; the commentators draw out what that definition means for assurance, and readers have long discussed how far such assurance extends.

**Related passages.** 2 Corinthians 5:7 sets the same contrast in daily terms: we walk by faith, not by sight [Cross-ref]. Romans 8:24–25 shows why hope and faith belong together, since hope that is seen is no hope at all, and we wait patiently for what we do not yet see [Cross-ref]. Hebrews 10:22 turns the same assurance into an invitation to draw near with a sincere heart in full assurance of faith [Cross-ref].

**Reflect.** Where in your life right now are you being asked to treat something you hope for as though it were already solid ground?

### Proverbs 31:10: Deeper study

**Biblical meaning.** This verse opens the acrostic poem that closes the book of Proverbs. It begins with a question—"who can find?"—implying that such a wife is rare, though not entirely wanting [JFB]. She is called a wife of "noble character," literally a woman of strength, meaning moral courage [JFB]. Her worth is set above rubies or jewels [JFB]. The verses that follow describe her: her husband's heart trusts her, he lacks nothing of value, and she brings him good, not harm, all her days.

**Historical context.** This description of the virtuous woman is designed to show what wives women should make and what wives men should choose [Henry]. It consists of twenty-two verses, each beginning with a letter of the Hebrew alphabet in order, which makes some think it was a poem by itself rather than part of the lesson Lemuel's mother taught him [Henry]. The picture is drawn in accordance with the customs of Eastern nations, but its moral teachings suit all times and places [JFB].

**Original language.** The word for "noble character" or "virtuous" is *chayil*, meaning strength or worthiness—here, moral strength rather than physical might [Lexicon]. The phrase rendered "who can find?" uses *matza*, "to find," implying such a woman is difficult to discover [Lexicon]. Her "price" is *mekher*, meaning merchandise or value, set far above *peninim*, jewels or rubies [Lexicon].

**Theological significance.** The verse presents a wife of strength and moral courage as a rare treasure [Henry]. Her value is measured not in wealth or appearance but in character—the same quality of strength used of able, God-fearing leaders [Henry]. Commentators note that such a woman contributes greatly to keeping up religion in families and passing it on to children [Henry]. The text itself simply asks who can find her; the conclusion that she is worth more than jewels is the writer's own estimate of her worth [JFB].

### Jeremiah 29:11: Deeper study

**Biblical meaning.** The verse sits inside a letter Jeremiah sends to exiles in Babylon. Just before it, God warns against prophets and diviners who were falsely promising a quick return (Jeremiah 29:8–9). Then God sets a different foundation: the exile will last seventy years, and only after that will He restore them (Jeremiah 29:10). Verse 11 explains why they can trust this: God knows His own plans for them — plans for well-being, not harm, giving them a future and a hope. The next verses show the response He expects: they will call on Him, pray, and seek Him wholeheartedly (Jeremiah 29:12–13) [Henry].

**Historical context.** God is speaking through the prophet Jeremiah to the Jews carried away into Babylon. False prophets among them were saying the captivity would be short, so the people should not settle in but stay ready to go back; God calls these prophecies lies spoken in His name (Jeremiah 29:8–9) [Henry]. Matthew Henry notes that the seventy years are reckoned from the first captivity, not the last, and that God's promise is to visit them in mercy and bring the exile to an end in His own time [Henry]. The people had swung between false confidence and despair [JFB].

**Original language.** *Machashavot* (מַחְשְׁבוֹת) means "plans" or "thoughts," here God's own purposes toward His people [Lexicon]. *Shalom* (שָׁלוֹם) is rendered "well-being" — peace in the sense of wholeness and good, not merely the absence of conflict [Lexicon]. *Acharit* (אַחֲרִית) means "a future" or "end"; the KJV's "expected end" reflects an end such as you would wish for, a happy outcome [JFB] [Lexicon].

**Theological significance.** The passage contrasts God's knowledge with that of the false prophets: "I alone," God says, know His purposes, while they "know nothing" of them though they pretend to [JFB]. A second theme is the reliability of God's timing. Henry observes that even when the church's deliverance does not come in our lifetime, it is enough that it comes in God's time, which is the best time [Henry]. The verse itself states God's intention toward the exiles; the commentaries draw out that this hope rested on His promise, not on their circumstances [Henry].

**Related passages.** Isaiah 55:8–12 is glanced at by the commentary: God's thoughts are not ours, and His word accomplishes what He sends it to do [JFB] [Cross-ref]. Psalm 33:11 echoes the same confidence — the counsel of the LORD stands forever [Cross-ref]. Lamentations 3:26 adds the posture this hope calls for: waiting quietly for the salvation of the LORD [Cross-ref].

**Reflect.** Where in your life are you tempted to trade God's patient timing for a quick, easy answer?


### Philippians 4:13: Explain easier

**Simple explanation.** Paul has just described learning to be content in every circumstance—humble or abundant, full or hungry, plenty or need (Philippians 4:11–12). Then he declares he has strength for all things, not only for those extremes [JFB]. The strength is not his own; it comes through his living union with the One who gives him power [JFB]. ### Philippians 4:13: Deeper study

**Biblical meaning.** Paul has just described learning contentment across every circumstance—humility and abundance, fullness and hunger, plenty and need (Philippians 4:11–12). Verse 13 is his summary of that whole list: not a general promise about any ambition, but his declaration that he has strength for all these situations. The Greek behind "I can do all things" carries the sense of having strength for all things [JFB]. The strength is not his own; it comes through the One who strengthens him, a living union with Christ [JFB] [Clarke].

**Historical context.** Paul writes this letter as a prisoner at Rome, and he is thanking the Philippians for a gift sent to support him [Henry]. He recalls their earlier generosity—how in the beginning of the gospel, when he left Macedonia, no church but theirs partnered with him in giving and receiving (Philippians 4:15) [Henry]. For a time they had not been able to send anything, and Paul excuses their delay rather than upbraiding them [Henry]. His contentment, then, is spoken by a man in need who is genuinely grateful.

**Original language.** The verb ἰσχύω (ischuō) means "I have strength" or "be strong," present active indicative, first person singular [Lexicon]. So the phrase is closer to "I have strength for all things" than to a bare "I can" [JFB]. The participle ἐνδυναμοῦντί (endunamounti) means "strengthening," describing the One who gives power [Lexicon]. The oldest manuscripts omit "Christ" here, so the sense is "in Him who gives me power" [JFB] [Clarke].

**Theological significance.** The theme here is strength received, not strength possessed. Paul says he was enabled "by the power of an indwelling Christ" [Clarke], and the oldest manuscripts point to "my living union and identification with Him, who is my strength" [JFB]. Interpreters differ on how wide the "all things" reaches: some read it as Paul's universal power declared after his specific examples of abundance and need [JFB], while others hear it as a general statement of grace for every situation. What the text itself says is that the strength is Christ's, given to Paul—not a claim about human capacity.

**Related passages.** 2 Corinthians 12:9–10 connects closely: God's power is perfected in weakness, so that when we are weak, then we are strong. Isaiah 40:29–31 promises power to the faint and renewed strength to those who wait on the LORD. Colossians 1:11 speaks of being strengthened with all power for endurance, patience, and joy.

**Reflect.** Where in your life are you trying to be strong on your own, and what would it look like to receive strength from Christ instead?


### Matthew 7:1: Explain easier

**Simple explanation.** Jesus is teaching His disciples how to treat other people's faults. Henry reads this as a caution against a proud, severe spirit that condemns everyone else while excusing oneself, the attitude he saw in the scribes and Pharisees [Henry]. The command is aimed at private persons, not at the lawful work of magistrates and ministers [Henry]. It forbids assuming authority over a brother, speaking evil of him, despising him, or judging rashly and uncharitably [Henry]. Judging another's heart, intentions, or eternal state belongs to God alone [Henry].

**Key takeaway.** Leave the judgment seat to God, and deal gently with your brother's faults rather than condemning him.


### Matthew 7:1: Deeper study

**Biblical meaning.** Matthew 7:1 opens a short unit on how disciples treat other people’s faults. The command “Do not judge” is followed by a reason: the same judgment and measure we use will be used on us [Henry]. Verse 3 then pictures a person noticing a speck in a brother’s eye while missing the beam in his own [Cross-ref]. The argument moves from a prohibition to a warning to an image of distorted vision, showing that harsh scrutiny of others rebounds on the one who judges.

**Historical context.** Jesus is speaking to his disciples in the Sermon on the Mount. Henry reads these words as a reproof of the scribes and Pharisees, who were severe and magisterial in condemning others while justifying themselves [Henry]. Henry notes that judging belongs to magistrates and ministers by office, but this caution is directed to private persons, to disciples, who must not set themselves up as judges over a brother [Henry].

**Original language.** The verb κρίνετε (krinete) means “to judge,” a present active imperative, second person plural — a command to stop or refrain from judging [Lexicon]. It is paired with κριθῆτε (krithēte), “you may be judged,” an aorist passive subjunctive, so the second half looks ahead to judgment received [Lexicon]. The negative particle μή (mē) simply negates the command [Lexicon].

**Theological significance.** Henry draws out two sides of “that ye be not judged.” First, those who presume to judge others may expect to be judged themselves — by people, and ultimately by God, before whom both parties must appear [Henry]. Second, those who are modest and charitable, who decline to judge others and judge themselves instead, will not be judged by the Lord [Henry]. The text itself states the warning; the commentary extends it into a contrast between censoriousness and mercy.

**Related passages.** Luke 6:37 repeats the command and adds “forgive, and you will be forgiven” [Cross-ref]. Romans 2:1–2 warns that judging another condemns yourself, since you do the same things [Cross-ref]. James 4:11–12 says the one who judges a brother speaks against the law, and that there is only one Lawgiver and Judge [Cross-ref].

**Reflect.** Where am I quickest to measure someone else by a standard I would not want applied to me?


### Isaiah 40:31: Explain easier

### Isaiah 40:31: Deeper study

**Biblical meaning.** Isaiah 40:31 closes a four-verse argument. Verses 28–29 declare that the LORD, the everlasting Creator, does not grow tired and gives power to the faint. Verse 30 concedes that even youths grow tired and stumble. Verse 31 then contrasts those who wait upon the LORD: they renew their strength and move through a descending sequence — mounting up like eagles, running without weariness, walking without fainting [JFB]. The commentary notes this climax moves from soaring to running to the ordinary act of walking, so that in every posture the waiting child of God is strong in the Lord [JFB].

**Historical context.** The sources provided do not identify the speaker, audience, or historical setting of this chapter. ### Genesis 1:1: Explain easier

**Simple explanation.** Genesis 1:1 opens the account of creation by naming the effect produced — the heavens and the earth, meaning the world and everything in it [Henry]. The visible creation is what is described here, not the angels [Henry]. The author and cause of it all is God; the Hebrew word Elohim speaks of power, and its plural form has long been understood by many learned and pious readers to suggest a plurality of persons in the Godhead [Henry][Clarke]. From what we see of heaven and earth, we may infer the eternal power of the Creator [Henry].

**Key takeaway.** In the beginning, God — the strong and good Creator — made the heavens and the earth.


### Genesis 1:1: Deeper study

**Biblical meaning.** Genesis opens with a single, sweeping statement: God created the heavens and the earth. The verse serves as an epitome, or summary, of the whole creation account that follows, with the next verses filling in the details [Henry]. It presents the visible world as a great house with upper and lower stories, wisely furnished in every room [Henry]. The writer mentions the heavens and the earth rather than angels, since his purpose is to account for the visible creation [Henry]. Light, sky, land and life unfold from this opening line.

The verb בָּרָא (ba.Ra') is qal perfect, third person masculine singular, "he created" [Lexicon]. The opening word בְּרֵאשִׁית (be.re.Shit) means "in beginning" [Lexicon]. The plural form Elohim has long been thought by learned and pious men to imply a plurality of Persons in the Divine nature, which is how the doctrine of the Trinity has been connected to this verse [Clarke].

**Theological significance.** The verse names God as the author and cause of the great work of creation [Henry]. The Hebrew word Elohim, a plural name speaking of him as many though he is one, has been understood by many as pointing to the plurality of persons in the Godhead, Father, Son, and Holy Ghost [Henry]. Clarke likewise notes that the plural form has long been supposed to imply a plurality of Persons in the Divine nature, confined to three Persons, from which the doctrine of the Trinity arose [Clarke]. These are interpretive conclusions drawn from the text rather than statements the verse itself makes.

### Exodus 14:14: Explain easier

**Simple explanation.** The Israelites were trapped between Pharaoh's army and the sea, and they were terrified, even saying they would rather have stayed in Egypt (Exodus 14:11–12). Moses tells them not to fear, to stand firm, and to watch the LORD's salvation (Exodus 14:13). In this verse he says the fighting belongs to God, not to them. Clarke puts it plainly: they would have no part in the honor of that day, because God alone would defeat their enemies [Clarke]. Their fearful complaints would be silenced, and they would see that no power can prevail against the LORD [Clarke]. Moses' own calm in this crisis came from hoping in God's intervention [JFB].

**Key takeaway.** When you cannot fight your own battle, God fights it for you; your part is to be still and trust Him.


### Exodus 14:14: Deeper study

**Biblical meaning.** The people are terrified at the sea, trapped between water and Egypt's army, and they accuse Moses of bringing them out to die (Exodus 14:11–12). Moses answers with a call not to fear, to stand firm, and to see the LORD's salvation, promising that the Egyptians they see today they will never see again (Exodus 14:13). Verse 14 states the heart of it: the LORD will fight for them, and they need only be still. The next verses show this stillness is not passivity—the LORD tells Moses to tell Israel to go forward, and to stretch out his staff so the sea divides (Exodus 14:15–16).

**Historical context.** Moses is speaking to the Israelites at the sea, with Pharaoh's army behind them. The people are a desperate, frightened crowd, and Moses is their leader in that crisis. [JFB] describes his composure here as one of the greatest examples of moral courage in history, severely tested by the danger around him and by a rebellious multitude. His courage, [JFB] says, came from seeing the miraculous cloud still with them and from hope in God's intervention—though he may have expected deliverance from any direction rather than the sea.

**Original language.** The verse names the LORD (Yahweh, the proper name of God), then says he "will fight" (yi.la.Chem, from the verb to fight, niphal imperfect, third person masculine singular)—the LORD himself is the one who fights. To the people it says "and you" (ve.'a.Tem, masculine plural pronoun) "will keep quiet" (ta.cha.ri.Shun, hiphil imperfect, second person masculine plural, meaning be quiet). The contrast is plain: he fights, they are quiet.

**Theological significance.** The verse holds two themes together. First, deliverance belongs to God: [Clarke] says the people would have no part in the honor of the day—God alone would bring them off and defeat their foes. Second, human fear is answered by quiet trust: [Clarke] reads "hold your peace" as their unbelieving fears and clamoring being confounded, so they would see that no might can prevail against the LORD, and that the feeblest take the prey when his power is exerted. The text states God fights for them; the commentators draw out that this leaves no room for their own boasting.

**Related passages.** Deuteronomy 3:22 and Deuteronomy 20:4 repeat the same promise—the LORD your God Himself goes with you and fights for you. 2 Chronicles 20:17 echoes the very wording here: take your positions, stand firm, see the salvation of the LORD, and do not be afraid. Isaiah 30:15 sounds a related note, that strength lies in quiet confidence.

### Joshua 1:9: Deeper study

**Biblical meaning.** God speaks directly to Joshua, repeating the charge to be strong and courageous. The command rests on God's presence, not on Joshua's own ability. In the surrounding verses, courage is tied to obeying the law Moses gave and meditating on it day and night, so that Joshua may prosper and succeed [Henry] [Clarke]. **Simple explanation.** In the middle of a chapter about love, Paul describes what love actually looks like in daily life. Then he lists what love refuses to do—it does not envy, does not boast, and is not puffed up with self-conceit [Henry][Clarke]. ### 1 Corinthians 13:4: Deeper study

**Biblical meaning.** In the middle of a letter about spiritual gifts, Paul turns to something greater than any gift. The verses just before this one say that tongues, prophecy, knowledge, faith, generosity and even bodily surrender gain nothing without love [1 Cor 13:1–3]. Verse 4 begins the description of what that love actually looks like. It opens with two positive marks, patience and kindness, then names what love does not do: it does not envy, does not boast, is not proud [JFB]. The list continues into the next verses, so this is the start of a portrait, not a single rule.

**Historical context.** Paul writes to the church at Corinth. The letter addresses a congregation where gifts like tongues were being used for display, and where party spirit and pride had taken hold [JFB]. Paul has just said that even remarkable gifts amount to nothing without love, and now he describes love's character. The comments note that "vaunteth not itself" is an indirect rebuke of those at Corinth who used the gift of tongues for mere display, and "not puffed up" speaks to party zeal, as some at Corinth were [JFB].

The negatives follow: *zēloi*, "is envious," from a verb meaning to be eager, which the notes say includes jealousy as well as envy [Lexicon][JFB]; *perpereuetai*, "is boastful," to boast [Lexicon]; and *phusioutai*, "is puffed up," to inflate [Lexicon].

**Theological significance.** The commentators treat these qualities as marks by which a person can examine whether this grace is present, and as reasons to desire it [Henry]. Patience is described as the negative side, enduring provocation from others, and kindness as the positive side, extending good to others [JFB]. Henry groups the rest under two headings: love suppresses envy, so that another's good is not a grief but a shared joy, and love subdues pride and vain-glory, so that it does not swell over its own gifts [Henry]. Clarke likewise says this love is not grieved when another has greater blessings, and rejoices in others' happiness as in its own [Clarke]. These are the writers' interpretive conclusions drawn from the text.

Colossians 3:12 tells believers to clothe themselves with compassion, kindness, humility, gentleness and patience, the same qualities in different words [Cross-ref]. ### Luke 1:38: Explain easier

**Simple explanation.** Mary has just heard that the Holy Spirit will come upon her and that the child will be called the Son of God, and that nothing God says ever fails [JFB]. Her answer is consent: she calls herself the Lord's servant and agrees to what the angel has told her [Clarke]. Clarke reads her words as fully crediting what was said and being ready to obey and to let God's purposes be accomplished in her, and he connects that moment of faith and intended obedience with the conception described in verse 35 [Clarke]. Then the angel leaves her [JFB].

**Key takeaway.** Mary receives God's word with trust and offers herself to serve it, leaving the outcome to him.


### Luke 1:38: Deeper study

**Biblical meaning.** The angel has just told Mary that the Holy Spirit will come upon her and that the child will be called the Son of God, backing it with Elizabeth's pregnancy and the assurance that no word from God fails [Luke 1:35–37]. Mary answers by naming herself the Lord's servant and consenting: "May it happen to me according to your word" [Luke 1:38]. Her reply follows the angel's reasoning rather than resisting it. The scene closes simply — the angel leaves her [Luke 1:38]. One commentator calls this "marvellous faith in such circumstances" [JFB].

**Historical context.** Mary, a young woman of Nazareth betrothed to Joseph, is speaking with the angel Gabriel, who has announced that she will bear a son [Luke 1:35–38]. She responds to the promise just given about the Holy Spirit, the power of the Most High, and Elizabeth's sixth-month pregnancy [Luke 1:35–36]. Her words are addressed to the angel as her answer. Immediately afterward she hurries to a town in the hill country of Judah and greets Elizabeth [Luke 1:39–40]. The angel departs once she has spoken [Luke 1:38].

**Original language.** *Doulē* (handmaid, slave) is the word Mary uses of herself — a female servant belonging to the Lord [Lexicon]. *Genoito* (would that it happen) is an optative verb, expressing her wish or consent rather than a command [Lexicon]. *Rhēma* (declaration) is the word rendered "word" — the angel's spoken message she accepts [Lexicon]. Together they show willing submission, not passive resignation.

**Theological significance.** Mary's answer joins full belief in what was said with readiness to obey, and one commentator holds that at this act of faith and purposed obedience the conception of the humanity of Jesus took place, "and it was Done unto her according to his word" [Clarke]. The text itself simply records her consent and the angel's departure [Luke 1:38]. A theme here is trusting that God is able to do what He has promised, the same persuasion credited to Abraham [Cross-ref, Romans 4:20–21]. Christians read Mary's role differently across traditions; the verse states her response, not a doctrine about her.

**Related passages.** Romans 4:20–21 connects through Abraham, who was fully persuaded that God was able to do what He had promised [Cross-ref]. Psalm 119:38 asks God to establish His word to His servant, echoing Mary's servant language [Cross-ref]. Psalm 116:16 repeats "I am Your servant," the same posture Mary takes [Cross-ref].

**Reflect.** Where in your own life is God asking for a "may it happen to me" rather than a negotiation?


### John 11:35: Explain easier

**Simple explanation.** Jesus arrives after Lazarus has died and sees Mary and the mourners weeping. The word for their crying suggests loud wailing for the dead, while the word used of Jesus means quiet tears [JFB]. John keeps the sentence very short, and one commentator calls it the least verse in the Bible yet inferior to none [Clarke]. Henry reads the tears as Christ's real sympathy with his grieving friends, and notes he was inwardly moved before this, groaning in spirit and troubled [Henry]. Some early writers thought weeping diminished Christ and even removed a passage about him weeping from Luke's Gospel [Clarke].

**Key takeaway.** Jesus' quiet tears show him truly sharing human sorrow, not standing apart from it [JFB] [Henry].


### John 11:35: Deeper study

**Biblical meaning.** Jesus arrives to find Mary and the Jews who came with her weeping over Lazarus's death. Moved in spirit and troubled, He asks where Lazarus has been laid, then weeps [JFB]. The verse follows His deep emotion at the tomb and precedes the crowd's response: some note how He loved Lazarus, while others wonder why He did not prevent the death [Cross-ref]. The Evangelist records this brief scene as a window into Christ's sympathy with grieving friends [Henry].

**Historical context.** Jesus is speaking with Mary, sister of Lazarus, and the Jews who came to comfort her after her brother's death [Henry]. Mary falls at His feet, expressing that He could have prevented Lazarus's death. The Jews with her are weeping for the loss of a good neighbour and friend [Henry]. Jesus asks where Lazarus has been placed, and they invite Him to come and see. The Evangelist writes this account some sixty years after the event [JFB].

**Original language.** The verb is ἐδάκρυσεν (edakrusen), meaning "to weep," an aorist active indicative, third person singular [Lexicon]. The subject is Ἰησοῦς (Iēsous), "Jesus," a nominative singular noun [Lexicon]. Commentators note this word denotes silent tears, distinct from the loud wailing used earlier for Mary and the Jews [JFB].

**Theological significance.** The verse shows Christ's real human emotion and His oneness with us in our stricken humanity [JFB]. Some commentators see His tears as more than sorrow for suffering and death, suggesting they also reflect the law that "the wages of sin is death" [JFB]. Others emphasize His tender sympathy with afflicted friends, sharing in their sorrows [Henry]. Some early writers thought weeping was beneath Christ's dignity, but the text presents it as fitting and significant [Clarke].

**Related passages.** Luke 19:41 records Jesus weeping over Jerusalem, another instance of His tears [Cross-ref]. Hebrews 4:15 speaks of a high priest who sympathizes with our weaknesses and was tempted yet without sin [Cross-ref]. John 11:33 describes Jesus being deeply moved and troubled when He saw Mary and the Jews weeping [Cross-ref].

**Reflect.** When you bring your grief to Jesus, what does it mean to you that He weeps alongside you?


### 1 Peter 5:7: Explain easier

**Simple explanation.** Peter has just urged humble submission to God's hand, and here he adds what flows from it: confident reliance on God's goodness, so that being free of care goes together with humble submission to Him [JFB]. The casting is decisive and done once for all, as the Greek tense shows [JFB]. The burden of care is lifted off the person and placed on God [JFB]. God concerns Himself with whatever touches His people, spiritual or temporal, great or small; what affects them affects Him [Clarke]. Peter echoes Psalm 55:22, where the burden is cast on the Lord who sustains [Clarke].

**Key takeaway.** Because God genuinely concerns Himself with everything that touches you, you can hand Him your anxiety and stop carrying it alone [Clarke].


### 1 Peter 5:7: Deeper study

**Biblical meaning.** Peter has been addressing humility among believers, urging younger men to submit to elders and all to clothe themselves with humility, since God opposes the proud but gives grace to the humble (1 Peter 5:5). Verse 6 calls for humbling ourselves under God's mighty hand. Verse 7 follows directly: casting all anxiety on Him flows out of that humble submission. The near context then turns to sober alertness against the devil (5:8-9). The argument moves from humility before God to releasing care onto Him, because He cares for His people. [Henry] notes that exemption from care accompanies humble submission to God, and that faith casts the burden of care off the person onto God. [Clarke] adds that whatever concerns a follower of God, whether spiritual or temporal, great or small, God concerns Himself with it.

**Historical context.** The apostle Peter writes to Christians who were already under very hard circumstances. [Henry] observes that Peter, knowing this, rightly supposes what follows. Having first addressed the pastors or spiritual guides of the church, he now instructs the flock how to behave toward their ministers and one another. [Henry] explains that he calls them "younger" as generally younger than their grave pastors, using the term to signify inferiority, and exhorts them to give due respect, reverence, and submission to their elders. The letter speaks into a setting of suffering, which the following verses about the devil and worldwide suffering confirm (1 Peter 5:8-9).

**Original language.** The lexicon gives μέριμναν (merimnan), a noun meaning anxiety or concern, in the accusative singular feminine: "Cast all your anxiety." The related verb μέλει (melei), present active indicative, means "there is care" or "to concern," used of God's care for us. [JFB] notes that this second Greek word for "care" is not as strong as the previous Greek word rendered "anxiety." The participle ἐπιρίψαντες (epiripsantes), aorist active, means "having cast" or "to throw on," which [JFB] reads as a once-for-all act.

**Theological significance.** Two themes stand out. First, God's care: the text grounds the command to cast anxiety on the fact that He cares for you. [Clarke] states that He who knows God cares for him need have no anxious cares about himself, and that God concerns Himself with whatever interests His followers. Second, humble submission: [Henry] teaches that consideration of God's omnipotent hand should make us humble and submissive, and that humbling ourselves under His hand is the next way to deliverance and exaltation. The text itself commands the casting; the commentaries draw out the connection between humility and reliance on God's goodness.

**Related passages.** Psalm 55:22 calls readers to cast their burden on the LORD, who will sustain them; [Clarke] and [JFB] both connect this verse to that psalm. Philippians 4:6 directs believers to present requests to God by prayer and petition with thanksgiving, addressing anxiety in practice. Matthew 6:25-26 points to the Father's care for birds as grounds for not worrying about life's needs.

**Reflect.** What anxiety are you carrying today that you have not yet handed over to the God who cares for you?


### Psalm 46:10: Explain easier

**Simple explanation.** Psalm 46 pictures a world of upheaval, yet the people of God are called to stop striving and to know that He is God. In its setting, the command is spoken to those who set themselves against Him and His people: leave off opposing Me and troubling My people, for I am over all for their safety [JFB]. The verse then looks outward, declaring that God will be exalted among the nations and over the earth. The surrounding verses name Him the LORD of Hosts, our fortress, who brings devastation, ends wars, and breaks weapons of war [JFB].

**Key takeaway.** Stop striving and know that God is over all, and He will be exalted among the nations.


### Psalm 46:10: Deeper study

**Biblical meaning.** Psalm 46 moves from God as refuge to God as the one who ends conflict. Verse 8 calls worshipers to "come, see the works of the LORD," and verse 9 describes Him making wars to cease, breaking the bow, shattering the spear, and burning the shields. Then verse 10 speaks: "Be still and know that I am God." The command follows the picture of God dismantling weapons, not preceding it. The verse closes with a twofold promise, "I will be exalted among the nations, I will be exalted over the earth," and verse 11 repeats the refrain that the LORD of Hosts is with us and the God of Jacob is our fortress [JFB] [Cross-ref].

**Historical context.** The psalm is a song of the sons of Korah, and the speaker in verse 10 is God Himself, addressing those who oppose Him and trouble His people. The setting is a world of war and siege, where nations rage and kingdoms fall, yet the city of God stands secure. The command to "be still" is set against that turmoil. The surrounding verses picture God as present with His people and as the fortress they return to in the refrain at verses 7 and 11 [JFB].

**Original language.** The first word, הַרְפּוּ (har.Pu), is a plural imperative from a verb meaning "to slacken," glossed "be quiet" [Lexicon]. Paired with it is וּדְעוּ (U.de.'u), "and know," a plural imperative of the verb "to know" [Lexicon]. Together they are a call to stop and to recognize. The verb אָרוּם (a.Rum), "I will be exalted," is first person singular, used twice, first "among the nations" and then "on the earth" [Lexicon].

**Theological significance.** The commentary reads "be still" as "Leave off to oppose Me and vex My people. I am over all for their safety," and points to Isaiah 2:11 and Ephesians 1:22 [JFB]. So the stillness is not merely calm; it is the ceasing of opposition before the God who is over all. The verse's own words promise that God will be exalted among the nations and over the earth [Lexicon]. The cross-references echo this: Ezekiel 38:23 says the nations will know that He is the LORD, and Isaiah 2:17 says the LORD alone will be exalted in that day [Cross-ref]. The text states God's exaltation; how and when that unfolds is where readers differ.

**Related passages.** Habakkuk 2:20 and Zechariah 2:13 both call all the earth to be silent before the LORD, matching the hush of "be still" [Cross-ref]. Psalm 100:3 joins "know" with knowing that the LORD is God and that we are His people [Cross-ref]. Isaiah 2:17 shares the theme of the LORD alone being exalted [Cross-ref].

**Reflect.** What would it look like this week to stop striving and let your knowledge of God shape how you face a situation you cannot control?


### Micah 6:8: Explain easier

**Simple explanation.** Micah 6:8 answers the questions of verses 6–7, where Israel asks what grand offering could make peace with God. The prophet says God has already shown them what is good, so the question need not be asked as if they had never heard [JFB]. What God requires is not endless sacrifice but justice, mercy, and humble walking with him [JFB]. Henry notes that convicted people ask what they must do to please God, and Clarke reads the three duties as giving God, neighbour, and self their due, loving kindness, and humbling ourselves before God [Henry][Clarke].

**Key takeaway.** God has already shown what he asks: do justly, love mercy, and walk humbly with him.


### Micah 6:8: Deeper study

**Biblical meaning.** Micah 6 opens with God's case against Israel, and the people respond with escalating offers: burnt offerings, year-old calves, thousands of rams, rivers of oil, even a firstborn child (Micah 6:6–7). The question is what could possibly satisfy God. Verse 8 answers that God has already made His will known: act justly, love mercy, and walk humbly with Him. The three requirements move from how we treat others to how we stand before God, showing that He values moral obedience over ritual performance [JFB].

**Historical context.** Micah prophesies to Israel and Judah during a time of injustice and empty religion. In this passage, God and Israel are presented as parties in a dispute, and the people ask how they might be reconciled to God [Henry]. They address the prophet, God's messenger, because he had convinced them of their guilt [Henry]. Their question assumes that costly sacrifices might secure God's favor, but the answer redirects them from ritual to a changed life.

**Original language.** The word *mishpat* (מִשְׁפָּט) means "justice" — strict equity in dealing with others [Lexicon]. *Chesed* (חֶסֶד) is "covenant loyalty" or "kindness," a steadfast love that goes beyond what is owed [Lexicon]. The phrase *hatznea* (הַצְנֵעַ) means "to be modest" or "to be humble," describing a lowly walk before God [Lexicon]. Together these words frame the verse's three requirements.

**Theological significance.** The verse presents moral duties as God's true requirement, with sacrifice as a means to that end rather than the end itself [JFB]. Justice and mercy are "moral duties" of everlasting obligation, while the positive ordinances of sacrifice were only designed with a view to them [JFB]. Walking with God implies constant prayer and watchfulness, a familiar yet humble converse with Him [JFB]. The text does not say these acts earn salvation; Clarke insists God never says doing justly merits eternal glory, but that this is the way all people should walk.

**Related passages.** Deuteronomy 10:12–13 echoes the call to fear God, walk in His ways, love Him, and serve Him wholeheartedly [Cross-ref]. Proverbs 21:3 and Hosea 6:6 both affirm that righteousness, justice, and mercy are more desirable to God than sacrifice [Cross-ref]. These passages reinforce Micah's point that God seeks transformed lives, not merely ritual offerings.

**Reflect.** Where in your daily life is God asking you to trade an outward gesture for genuine justice, mercy, or humility?


### Ephesians 5:22: Explain easier

**Simple explanation.** This verse sits inside a longer teaching on how Christians relate to one another, beginning with the call in verse 21 to submit to one another out of reverence for Christ [Henry]. Wives are told to submit to their own husbands, and "your own" matters—it is not a stranger but the husband she is joined to [JFB]. The submission is done "as unto the Lord": she submits under Christ's eye, and so renders it to Christ Himself [JFB]. Henry notes it includes honouring and obeying, done from love and out of conscience toward God [Henry]. Clarke reads it as God's order, with the Church's submission to Christ as the pattern [Clarke].

**Key takeaway.** A wife's submission is offered to her own husband as an act done before the Lord, not to a stranger.


### Ephesians 5:22: Deeper study

**Biblical meaning.** This verse sits inside a chain of instructions that begins with the call for all believers to submit to one another out of reverence for Christ [Henry]. Paul then moves from that general rule to specific relationships, starting with wives and husbands, and he treats the church's relation to Christ as the pattern underneath it [JFB]. The wife's submission is directed to her own husband and is described as being done "as unto the Lord," so it is rendered under Christ's eye and so to Christ Himself [JFB]. The following verses supply the reason: the husband is head of the wife as Christ is head of the church [Henry].

**Historical context.** The writer is Paul, and he is addressing Christian households, turning from the shared life of the church to the duties within the home [Henry]. He speaks of these duties "in a Christian manner," setting the church's subjection to Christ as the example for the wife and Christ's love as the example for the husband [Henry]. The instruction is not framed as a stand-alone rule but as part of a larger exhortation to mutual yielding that Paul himself modeled [Henry].

**Original language.** The wives are addressed directly as *gunaikes* ("wives," vocative plural) [Lexicon]. The word *idiois* means "to your own," which the commentary takes as an argument for submissiveness: it is not a stranger, but your own husband [JFB][Lexicon]. The verb itself is not repeated in the oldest manuscripts; it is supplied from the previous verse, "submitting yourselves" [JFB].

**Theological significance.** Two themes stand out. First, submission here is religious before it is social: it is done "as unto the Lord," under Christ's eye, and the husband stands to the wife in the relation the Lord does to the church [JFB]. Second, the headship language is tied to Christ's saving work, since Christ's authority over the church is exercised for her good, and the husband is likewise to be employed for his wife's protection and comfort [Henry]. The commentaries also note that submission to the husband is inferior in kind and degree to what the wife owes Christ [JFB], and that "submit" is used of wives while "obey" is used of children, since there is greater equality between wives and husbands [JFB]. Christians read this passage differently today, and the sources themselves reflect older assumptions about household order.

**Related passages.** 1 Peter 3:1–6 also addresses wives submitting to their husbands, with attention to the inner disposition of the heart [Cross-ref]. Titus 2:5 lists submission to one's own husband among household duties, tied to the word of God not being discredited [Cross-ref]. Genesis 3:16 speaks of the woman's desire and the husband's rule, which the commentary connects to this submission [JFB][Cross-ref].

**Reflect.** How does reading "as unto the Lord" shape the way you hear this instruction?


### 1 Timothy 2:12: Explain easier

**Simple explanation.** Paul is addressing how women in the church should relate to learning and teaching. He does not permit a woman to teach or to take authority over a man, and says she is to remain quiet. The word behind "exercise authority" carries the sense of lording it over someone, or being an autocrat [JFB]. Henry reads the surrounding verses as a call to modesty, learning, and submission, noting that teaching is an office of authority [Henry]. Clarke records that women were barred from public offices under Roman law, and that this silence meant they were not to ask questions in public assemblies as men could [Clarke].

**Key takeaway.** Paul restricts women from teaching or holding authority over men in the church, calling them instead to a quiet, submissive posture.


### 1 Timothy 2:12: Deeper study

**Biblical meaning.** In this section Paul addresses how women in the church should live and learn. Verse 11 says a woman must learn quietly and submissively; verse 12 then says Paul does not permit a woman to teach or to exercise authority over a man, and she is to remain quiet. The reason follows in verses 13–14: Adam was formed first, then Eve, and the woman was the one deceived. The argument moves from learning, to teaching and authority, to creation order and the first transgression [Henry].

**Historical context.** Paul writes this letter to Timothy, who is leading the church at Ephesus. The instructions come within a passage about worship and conduct in the church, beginning with how women should dress and continuing to learning and teaching. Paul grounds his instruction in Genesis, referring to Adam being formed first and to Eve being deceived. Matthew Henry notes that women in the apostolic days were also notable professors of Christianity, and that the rules of Christianity reach women as well as men [Henry].

**Original language.** Two words carry the weight here. *didaskein* means "to teach," a present active infinitive [Lexicon]. *authentein* means "to use authority over," with the dictionary sense "to domineer" [Lexicon]. Jamieson, Fausset & Brown render it "to lord it over the man," literally "to be an autocrat" [JFB]. The word *hēsuchia* means "quietness" [Lexicon]. So the verse pairs teaching with a domineering kind of authority, and closes with quietness.

**Life in biblical times.** Adam Clarke describes the Roman legal setting: women were "precluded from all public offices," could not be judges or magistrates, could not sue or plead or act as proxies [Clarke]. He also notes that men in public assemblies were free to ask questions or interrupt a speaker, but this liberty was not granted to women [Clarke].

**Theological significance.** Two themes stand out. First, creation order: Adam was formed first, then Eve, which Henry reads as denoting her subordination and dependence [Henry]. Second, the fall: the woman was first in the transgression, though Henry adds the comfort that the sentence is no bar to acceptance with Christ for those who continue in faith, charity, and holiness [Henry]. Christians read this passage differently. Some take Paul's words as a permanent rule for the church; others read them as addressing a specific situation in Ephesus. The text itself states the prohibition and grounds it in Genesis; how broadly it applies is an interpretive conclusion, not something the verse itself settles.

**Related passages.** 1 Corinthians 14:34 says women are to be silent in the churches, not permitted to speak, but to be in submission, as the law says [Cross-ref]. It uses similar language of silence and submission, and is often read alongside this verse. Clarke cross-references it when explaining what "in silence" meant in the assemblies [Clarke].

### Judges 4:4: Explain easier

**Simple explanation.** Deborah is introduced right after Israel cried out to God under Jabin's harsh oppression [Jdg 4:3]. She judged Israel during the oppression, and the people came to her for judgment [Jdg 4:4-5].

**Key takeaway.** God raised up Deborah, a prophetess, to lead and judge Israel in a time of oppression.


### Judges 4:4: Deeper study

**Biblical meaning.** Judges 4 opens with Israel doing evil after Ehud died, and the LORD giving them into Jabin's hand; his commander Sisera had nine hundred chariots of iron, and Jabin oppressed Israel harshly for twenty years [JFB]. Into this distress, verse 4 introduces Deborah: a prophetess, the wife of Lappidoth, judging Israel at that time [JFB]. The following verses show her holding court under the Palm of Deborah between Ramah and Bethel, where Israelites came up to her for judgment, and from there summoning Barak [Henry]. Her leadership is presented as the preparation of the people for their deliverance [Henry].

**Historical context.** Israel is under Jabin king of Canaan, who reigned in Hazor, with Sisera commanding his forces from Harosheth-hagoyim; the people cry out because of the iron chariots and twenty years of oppression [JFB]. Deborah judges Israel during that oppression, and Henry suggests that, being a woman, she may have been the more easily permitted by the oppressor to do it [Henry]. She did not rule as a princess by civil authority, but as a prophetess and God's mouth to the people, correcting abuses and redressing grievances, especially those relating to the worship of God [Henry].

**Original language.** The verse names her נְבִיאָה (ne.vi.'Ah), "a prophetess," a feminine noun [Lexicon]. She is אֵשֶׁת (E.shet) לַפִּידוֹת (la.pi.Dot), "the wife of Lappidoth" [Lexicon]. The verb שֹׁפְטָה (sho.fe.Tah) is a feminine singular qal participle from "to judge," so she is described as "judging" Israel at that time [Lexicon]. The same word for "woman" or "wife" ('i.Shah) stands alongside the title, holding her role and her household together in one line [Lexicon].

**Theological significance.** Deborah is described as a prophetess, one instructed in divine knowledge by the immediate inspiration of the Spirit of God, with gifts of wisdom she did not attain in an ordinary way; she heard the words of God and probably saw the visions of the Almighty [Henry]. JFB likewise calls her a woman of extraordinary knowledge, wisdom, and piety, who acquired extensive influence and was held in universal respect, becoming the animating spirit of the government and discharging all the duties of a judge except that of military leader [JFB]. The text presents God raising up a prophetess to prepare His people for deliverance; how her authority relates to later teaching on leadership is a matter on which Christians read the passage differently.

**Related passages.** Exodus 15:20 calls Miriam a prophetess, showing another woman in Israel's story given that title [Cross-ref]. 2 Kings 22:14 names Huldah the prophetess, the wife of Shallum, consulted in Jerusalem [Cross-ref]. Luke 2:36 introduces Anna, a prophetess of the tribe of Asher [Cross-ref]. Together they show the title recurring across Israel's history.

### Galatians 3:28: Explain easier

**Simple explanation.** Paul has just said that all who believe are sons of God, clothed with Christ in baptism. Now he says the old divisions no longer count. Jamieson, Fausset and Brown read this as saying that in this sonship by faith no class is privileged above another, the way Jews once stood above Gentiles [JFB]. Clarke says all distinctions are done away as either helping or hindering; all are equally welcome to Christ and equally need him, and believers of every background and sex become one family through him [Clarke]. The word "one" is masculine in Greek, "one man," pointing to one new man in Christ [JFB].

**Key takeaway.** In Christ, differences of background, status and sex give no one an advantage and exclude no one from equal standing.


### Galatians 3:28: Deeper study

**Biblical meaning.** Paul has just told the Galatians that faith has come and they are no longer under a guardian [JFB]. In verses 26–27 he says they are all sons of God through faith and have clothed themselves with Christ. Verse 28 then draws out what that means: the old divisions between Jew and Greek, slave and free, male and female no longer set people apart, because all are one in Christ Jesus. Verse 29 completes the thought—belonging to Christ makes them Abraham's seed and heirs of the promise. The argument moves from sonship, to unity, to inheritance.

**Historical context.** Paul is writing to the churches in Galatia, a group of Gentile believers who were being pressured to add observance of the law to their faith in Christ. The letter argues that justification comes by faith, not by works of the law. In this section Paul is showing what faith in Christ has produced in them: a new identity as God's sons and a shared inheritance with Abraham, which the law had kept the Gentiles from.

**Original language.** The verse uses οὐκ ἔνι (ouk eni), "there is not," three times—a strong way of saying these categories simply do not exist in Christ. Ἕλλην (Hellēn) means "Greek," used here for the Gentile or heathen [Clarke]. δοῦλος (doulos) means "slave," paired with ἐλεύθερος (eleutheros), "free." The closing phrase uses εἷς (heis), "one," a masculine adjective—one man, one new man in Christ [JFB].

**Life in biblical times.** The sources describe disparities under the law: a man could shave his head and tear his clothes in mourning, impose a Nazirite vow on his son, be shorn for his father's vow, betroth or sell his daughter—a woman could do none of these [Clarke]. Males alone bore circumcision, the sign of the covenant, and alone could be kings and priests [JFB]. Women were treated more like children than adults and were not permitted to assemble with the men in the synagogues [Clarke].

**Theological significance.** The text says that in Christ there is no class privileged above another, as Jews had been above Gentiles under the law [JFB]. It says difference of sex makes no difference in Christian privileges: all of either sex are now "kings and priests unto God," and baptism is applied to male and female alike, unlike circumcision [JFB]. Clarke reads this as equal rights, equal privileges, and equal blessings under Christianity [Clarke]. The verse states the unity; how far it should be applied to particular roles and structures is where Christians read the passage differently, and the sources themselves do not settle that question.

**Related passages.** 1 Corinthians 12:12–13 uses the same three pairs—Jews or Greeks, slave or free—to describe being baptized into one body. Colossians 3:11 similarly says there is no Greek or Jew, slave or free, but Christ is all and in all. Romans 10:12–15 grounds the lack of difference between Jew and Greek in the one Lord who gives richly to all who call on Him.

**Reflect.** Where do you still treat someone as outside the "one" that Paul describes here?
